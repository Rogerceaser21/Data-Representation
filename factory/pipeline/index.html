The big flow

Setup to closeout, in one line of sight

Every stage runs in order. The four gold diamonds are where the pipeline stops and waits for a human answer, and gate 4 sits before the push, not after it. The two red loops are the automatic retries, the places the pipeline throws its own work away without being asked. Any gate answer can do the same thing: a rejection at a diamond sends that stage round again too.

Where the pipeline stops

The four gates

Four stages stop and wait. Everything else runs start to finish without stopping. These four are where the result gets steered: the words, the pace, the music, and whether it goes live.

Gate 1

Stage 1, Script

What Igor decides
Whether these are the exact words the video will say out loud, before any audio exists
What lands in front of you
The full narration text in chat, one block per scene, with the voice and the direction note. Answer it and the recording exists about a minute later
What a good answer contains
Either a plain yes, or the specific lines to change and what they should say instead
Cost of changing your mind
Before this gate: free, it is a text edit. After it: the whole recording is made again from scratch, rescored, every animation cue re-timed to the new words, the video re-rendered and the music re-cut to the new length. Hours, not minutes.

Gate 2

Stage 3, Cut and pace

What Igor decides
Whether a slightly rushed delivery is worth a shorter video
What lands in front of you
Both cuts as audio you can actually play, each with its real duration, not two numbers to imagine
What a good answer contains
A pick from the real options presented, with real durations attached, for example natural pace at 3m15 versus 10 percent faster at 3m00
Cost of changing your mind
After this gate: the audio is recut and every animation cue moves with it, so the video is re-rendered and the music re-cut. No new recording though, so it is cheaper than gate 1.

Gate 3

Stage 7, Music

What Igor decides
The emotional register of the whole video, meaning the mood family (cinematic, upbeat, minimal tech pulse), not just a track
What lands in front of you
Three 30 second preview files sent directly, each already mixed under the real narration and labelled with its register and track name. Answer it and the final render is roughly the video's own running time away
What a good answer contains
The name of one of the three previews; the useful precedent is v1.4, where "yoga class" rejected the register itself and rewrote the brief
Cost of changing your mind
After this gate: a new bed is generated or picked, mixed and baked to the full length, and the video re-rendered. The narration is untouched, so this is the cheapest of the three to redo.

Gate 4

Stage 9, Ship

What Igor decides
Decided at the start, not at the end. The default is that the approved plan runs all the way to live without stopping
What lands in front of you
Nothing at this point, because it was agreed up front. You do not watch the finished video before it goes live unless you asked to. To get a viewing, say the sentence in the cheat sheet at the plan stage: "Build it and stop before the push, I want to see it first."
What a good answer contains
A plain statement of whether the approved plan ends with publication
Cost of changing your mind
Asking for a viewing up front costs nothing. Asking after the push means the video is already public, so a change is a new version and a re-publish.

0 Setupclone the known good sample No Igor gate

What happens

Claude asks one set of questions up front: the topic, who it is for, which product or data it shows, which code repository it ships from, and whether it also gets a web page to sit on. It then copies the frozen worked sample (the Staff Voice video, v1.5) into a temporary working folder and strips out the old voice track, so what remains is a working shell. The sample is used because it is known good: it already passes the lint, layout, motion and contrast checks, and every wiring detail is correct in it. The look is seeded from the house brand file, and the video is built from the real product's stylesheet so it shows the actual product rather than a mock-up.

Tools that run

  • cp -R sample-staff-voice/staff-voice-src copies the frozen worked sample as starting point
  • cp template/scripts/score_take.py scripts/ adds the voice-quality scorer separately
  • rm -f assets/narration.* timings.json narration.srt deletes the old video's voice files
  • template/STARTER.md written instructions for this clone-and-swap

What can go wrong

  • Building inside Google Drive. Drive reverts edits made in place, so all work happens in a temporary session folder and is copied back later.
  • Forgetting the scorer. It is not inside the sample project and has to be copied in from the template, or stage 4 has nothing to run.
  • Trying to install packages. This pipeline never installs anything locally; every tool runs on demand at a pinned version.

Gate

No Igor gate, and nothing to verify here. Runs start to finish on its own.

1 Scriptthe words and the real numbers Gate 1

What happens

Claude writes the narration into a single file, one entry per scene, along with the chosen voice and a direction note for how it should be read. Length is budgeted by word count: roughly 550 words gives three minutes at a natural reading speed. Any number that appears on screen is calculated by running the product's own code against its own saved data, never typed in by hand; anything that cannot be sourced that way is labelled on screen as proposed or illustrative. Teacher names are always invented.

Tools that run

  • scenes.json the script file, edited directly
  • git show origin/main:dashboard/src/index.html pulls the product's real, current code

What can go wrong

  • Reading the product code from the wrong copy. The everyday working copy is deliberately out of date by months, so the numbers must come from the published version.
  • Words that change meaning depending on stress (present, live, read, record, project) get the wrong reading from the voice engine, and because the voice is generated in one pass that costs a full re-record. These are reworded before any audio is made.
  • Em dashes and en dashes in on-screen text, which house style forbids everywhere. Ordinary hyphens are fine.

Gate

GATE 1: Igor approves the exact wording before any audio is generated. He is deciding whether these are the words the video will say, out loud, permanently. A good answer is either a plain yes, or the specific lines he wants changed and what they should say instead. Versions have been rejected before for wording changes he did not sanction, and he does check.

2 Takeone request, one take No Igor gate

What happens

The whole approved script is sent to the voice engine in one single request, with three-second pauses marking where scenes break. One complete recording of the whole script, made in one request, is called a take (it is the unit that gets accepted or thrown away, never a single line). Sending it whole is what keeps the voice consistent from start to finish. The script measures how fast the result actually speaks, ignoring silences, and if it is off target it asks again with a firmer instruction to slow down, up to five attempts. It takes about a minute and costs a few cents.

Tools that run

  • node scripts/tts-single.mjs generates the entire narration in one pass

What can go wrong

  • The voice engine cuts off mid-script, or races. The script detects both and rejects the take rather than passing it on.
  • Generating line by line, or stretching individual lines to fit. Both are banned outright; they are what made earlier versions sound like different people between scenes.
  • Reopening the question of which voice engine to use. A local engine was trialled on 2026-08-03 and lost; Gemini with the Charon narrator stays until there is new evidence.
Engineering rules, for the record
  • Two whole versions were rejected because the narrator "sounded like two different people": each line had been generated in its own request, and Gemini re-derives the speaker identity on every request. Rule now: generate the ENTIRE script in one request, split it locally, never regenerate a single line.
  • The per-line generator scripts (tts.mjs, fit.mjs) were deleted outright, because keeping them around meant somebody would eventually "just redo one line" for a typo. Rule: to change a line, edit scenes.json and regenerate the whole take.
  • Setting temperature/topP/topK on the TTS call does nothing for determinism (Vertex ignores them for TTS) and low values made the model produce one sentence then silence. Rule: never set them on a TTS call.
  • The 2026-08-03 local-engine trial (Voicebox) burned a session: cloned voices matched pitch range but read 25 to 30 percent too fast with weak sentence stops, the only steering field (instruct) crashed the app's backend, and Chatterbox Turbo's 9 GB model swamped a 16 GB Mac.
  • The Gemini key lives outside the project and has to stay out of transcripts. Rule: the script reads it from ~/AIS-Data-Dashboard/.env itself, never echo, print or copy it into a project file.
  • Sources: tts-voice-law.md, gotchas.md.

Gate

No Igor gate, and nothing to verify here. Runs start to finish on its own.

3 Cut and pacesplit, tighten, deflutter Gate 2

What happens

The single long recording is cut into scenes at its longest silences. Two speed corrections are applied, in this order. First one global adjustment across the whole recording, snapped to none if it is under 3 percent. Then a safety cap, scene by scene, that can only ever slow a scene down: any scene reading faster than 192 words a minute is pulled back to that ceiling, never below 0.84 of its speed, and corrections under 3 percent snap to none. The cap exists because the voice engine accelerates through a long take, measured at 141 words a minute at the cover ramping to 231 at the tail, which Igor heard as the narrator speeding up and falling off.

What the one-take law bans is stretching lines that were generated separately, because that smears the voice into two different people. This is one performance, cut into pieces and only ever slowed, so the speaker cannot drift. The capped take measured better than the uncapped one, not worse.

A second pass then trims the dead air inside each scene by removing the middle of each pause and keeping the edges, which typically takes about 28 seconds off a four-minute recording. A third pass evens out slow tonal drift across the recording, and runs exactly once, because running it twice applies the correction twice.

Tools that run

  • node scripts/split.mjs cuts scenes, sets one global speed, then applies the slow-down-only per-scene pace cap (192 wpm ceiling, 0.84 floor)
  • python3 scripts/tighten.py shortens the pauses inside scenes
  • python3 scripts/deflutter.py evens out slow tone drift

What can go wrong

  • Misreading the speed factor. The setting multiplies speed rather than setting it, so the number the script prints is the one to trust, not the target written in the code.
  • Hand-editing the timing files the scripts produce. They are regenerated by the scripts and hand edits are lost or break sync.
  • Running the drift correction twice on the same cut, which double-applies it.
  • "Fixing" the per-scene pace cap back to a single global factor. It looks like a violation of the one-take law and is not; removing it brings back the narrator who speeds up and falls off.
Engineering rules, for the record
  • v1.3 applied a +10.6 percent global speed-up and the client heard it instantly as "sped up". Rule: natural recorded pace ships, and split.mjs snaps any tempo correction under 3 percent to exactly 1.0.
  • v1.1 stretched each line by a different factor (0.86 to 1.15); that smeared the voice by differing amounts per scene and IS the two-narrators bug. Rule: never vary a factor across lines that were generated separately. The per-scene cap added in factory-v1.1 is a different thing: one performance, slow-down only, PACE_CAP 192 wpm, floor 0.84, sub-3 percent snaps to none. The capped take scored better, not worse (ratio 1.00, adj_mean 0.89).
  • The atempo factor was written backwards three separate times in one session: going from 192 wpm to 167 wpm needs 167/192 = 0.87, not the other way up. Rule: read the factor split.mjs prints next to the measured wpm before rendering.
  • deflutter.py rewrites the narration file in place and has no self-guard, so running it twice double-applies the tone correction (tighten.py protects itself with a "tightened" flag, deflutter does not). Rule: run it after any recut and exactly once, and track that yourself.
  • TARGET_WPM counts pauses inside a scene as speech, so two takes with the same number land at different lengths. Rule: trust the master length split.mjs prints, not the constant.
  • Sources: tts-voice-law.md (one-take law and the pace rules), the pace-cap comment in template/scripts/split.mjs, handoff 2026-07-29.

Gate

GATE 2: pace against length is Igor's call. He is deciding whether a slightly rushed delivery is worth a shorter video. A good answer picks one of the real options presented, with real durations attached, for example natural pace at 3m15 versus 10 percent faster at 3m00. On v1.3 he heard a 10.6 percent speed-up and rejected it, and v1.4 removed it, so the trade-off is always put to him with the actual numbers rather than described vaguely.

4 Score the voicenumbers decide, not opinion Verification gate

What happens

Before anything is built around this recording, the voice is measured for consistency. Two scripts compare how different each scene sounds from the scene next to it, against how much natural variation exists inside a single scene. If the gap between neighbouring scenes is smaller than the recording's own natural wobble, the take is consistent enough to keep. If not, the whole take is thrown away and regenerated from stage 2.

Tools that run

  • python3 scripts/voiceconsist.py measures scene-to-scene voice consistency
  • python3 scripts/score_take.py prints one line of pass numbers

What can go wrong

  • Shipping a take nobody measured. This is banned: the audible register jump the score catches is exactly what got v1.0 and v1.1 rejected by ear.
  • Assuming the drift correction fixed it. The v1.3 first take still measured an audible register shift after correction and had to be regenerated.
  • Skipping the measurement because the last take was fine. Explicitly forbidden; it is one minute and one API call to check.
Engineering rules, for the record
  • In v1.3 the first take measured 3.62 dB (decibels, the unit for how much louder or different two sounds are) of voice difference between two neighbouring scenes even after the tone fix, the exact audible register jump that got v1.0 and v1.1 rejected. It was thrown away, the next take measured 1.16 dB and shipped.
  • Accepting on a hunch is banned: accept only when the scene-to-scene difference sits below the take's own phonetic floor (about 1.6 dB on this script, meaning how much the same voice differs from itself on different words), no single pair spikes to 2 or 3 times the average, and the ratio is at or under about 1.1.
  • A take reading faster than 210 wpm (words per minute) is not a fast reader, it is a truncated take. Rule: reject it.
  • Source: tts-voice-law.md, "The take-scoring gate".

Gate

No Igor gate; a mandatory verification gate. The pipeline does not stop for a human here, but it does not pass either unless the numbers say so: score_take.py decides accept against regenerate, and skipping it is forbidden.

5 Composecues pinned to the narration No Igor gate

What happens

The visuals are written into a template file and then generated into the finished page; the generated file is never edited directly. Every animation is pinned to the narration timings, so visuals land on the words. Early movements are pinned to a fixed number of seconds, and anything landing two seconds or later into a line is pinned to a proportion of that line instead, so a shorter line squeezes its animation rather than losing the end of it. Highlights that ring and lift part of the screen are rationed to five per three-minute video, and the video ends with a silent eight-second end card.

Tools that run

  • node scripts/build.mjs generates the final composition file
  • npm run check validates the composition automatically
  • npx hyperframes check --at 150,168,181,196 inspects specific late timing moments

What can go wrong

  • Editing the generated file instead of the template. The next build overwrites it.
  • Adding the end card without re-baking the audio to the new total length, in which case the card plays silent.
  • Motion that leaves things hidden. Movement is decoration only; the resting state has to be the finished, visible state.
Engineering rules, for the record
  • Cue edits made directly in the generated index.html worked in preview and vanished on the next build. Rule: edit template/composition.html and re-run the build; timings.json (the file that records where every scene starts and how long it runs) and the subtitle file are generated too, never hand-edited.
  • The v1.2 to v1.3 recut shortened every scene from a 240s to a 180s video, and every hardcoded cue offset broke silently: at('s08_kpi2', 19.1) inside a 15s scene fires after the scene is already gone. Rule: entrances under 1.5s stay absolute, anything at 2.0s or later converts to the proportional atf() form so a shorter line compresses its choreography instead of losing its tail.
  • A six-item stagger at 2.1s spacing ran 12.6s and overran its scene ("bit us"). Rule: staggers keep fixed spacing (scaling them reads as a speed change), but always check the last item still lands inside the scene.
  • A fromTo that is not an element's first tween makes GSAP stamp the FROM state at time zero, so the element sits visibly wrong until its cue fires. Rule: carry immediateRender:false on those, and see miss 14 for what happens when it is carried on the wrong tween.
  • Freeze-safe law, learned from the dashboard: if the timeline never runs (backgrounded tab, a seek landing mid-render) the frame must still read as finished, so entrances use .from() and the resting DOM already carries the real value and label. Star fields use a seeded generator, never Math.random, or the render is not reproducible.
  • Landing a two-column card column by column reads as broken. Rule: land it as one unit.
  • Sources: motion-rules.md, gotchas.md.

Gate

No Igor gate, and nothing to verify here. Runs start to finish on its own.

6 Render and verifyframes pulled, audio measured Verification gate

What happens

The video is rendered to a finished file, which takes roughly as long as the video's own running time. Nothing is then taken on trust: still frames are pulled out at the cover, at each highlight and at the end card and actually looked at, and the loudness of specific stretches of audio is measured rather than assumed. Frames are also pulled from inside the animation windows, not only where everything has settled, because an animation can look correct at rest and blink blank halfway through.

You do not see the finished video at this point unless you asked for it. Verifying here means Claude checking pulled frames and measured audio levels, not a viewing by you. To get a viewing before it goes live, say so at the plan stage.

Tools that run

  • npm run render renders the finished video file
  • ffmpeg -frames:v 1 extracts single still frames for checking
  • ffmpeg -af volumedetect measures loudness of an audio stretch

What can go wrong

  • Watching the render log for a completion message that never appears, because the renderer holds its output back. The finished file appearing in the renders folder is the real signal.
  • Declaring it correct without pulling frames. Verification is by extracted frame and measured audio, never by assertion.
  • Checking only the settled moments. An animation can sit correct before and after its beat and still blink blank in the middle of it.
Engineering rules, for the record
  • npm run render buffers its output, so a watcher tailing the log for "Render complete" waits forever, and renders/ already contains older files. Rule: render in the background and wait for a NEW mp4 to appear, budgeting about 1.1x realtime.
  • A 420px-wide screenshot looked clipped and sent someone chasing an overflow bug that did not exist: headless Chrome floors its layout width at 500px. Rule: measure (scrollWidth, hyperframes check), do not diagnose from a narrow screenshot.
  • clip_media_fit warnings fired on every build and look like failures; they are the normal state whenever audio is shorter than its window (lead, tail, silent end card). Rule: do NOT "fix" them by stretching audio, that reintroduces the two-narrators bug.
  • Contrast warnings inside components lifted verbatim from the product's own CSS are pre-existing and out of scope. Rule: diff against the previous run and only react to NEW warnings from your own scenes.
  • After a recut, clips collided by a hundredth of a second and lint rejected the whole composition. Rule: round scene starts first, then derive each duration from the rounded values so scenes abut exactly.
  • A duplicate-composition lint error with no obvious cause was a second root-level file carrying data-composition-id. Rule: exactly one composition file at the root, ever.
  • Verification traps repeat often enough that the standing rule is: prove it from the shipped artifact (pull frames, measure audio levels, run the score script), because build, typecheck and lint passes are not visual or audible proof.
  • Source: gotchas.md.

Gate

No Igor gate; a mandatory verification gate. Nothing here waits for a human answer, but nothing moves on either until the proof exists: pulled frames, including frames from inside the animation windows, and measured audio levels.

7 Musiclibrary first, generate second Gate 3

What happens

First the factory music library is checked. Three takes (cine1, drive3, tech3) are already paid for and approved for reuse, so a video in one of those registers (register meaning the mood family: cinematic, upbeat, minimal tech pulse) skips generation entirely and goes straight to the mix. Generation only runs for a register the library does not cover.

When it does run, the music is generated to order rather than hunted for in stock libraries: three written briefs, one per register, are fed to a music model at about eight cents a take.

What every candidate must pass before you hear it

  • Actual rhythmic movement. Flat pads score zero on this and read as a yoga class.
  • A steady body between its fade in and its fade out, so it does not swell in the middle of a sentence.
  • Its energy sitting clear of the frequency band the voice occupies.
  • No sung vocals, because words under words are unlistenable.
  • At least as long as the video, so no loop seam has to be hidden.

The survivors are then mixed under the same 30 second slice of the real narration, with a music-only hold inside the slice, so he compares like with like and can actually hear the bed lift.

Tools that run

  • template/scripts/lyria-sweep.py generates music takes to brief
  • knowledge/music-register-briefs.json the three written register briefs
  • ffmpeg loudnorm and amix mixes music under real narration

What can go wrong

  • Going back to free libraries. Two rounds were rejected outright, and one of the sources is blocked to automated access anyway.
  • Picking by ear alone before measuring. Ambient pads consistently fail the movement measurement and were the source of the "makes me fall asleep" verdict.
  • Mixing the music so quietly and so flatly that it cannot be heard at all, which is what happened on the first attempt.
Engineering rules, for the record
  • v1.4 shipped an ambient pad that satisfied the written brief perfectly and Igor rejected it in one line: "yoga class". Rule: the brief is quiet MOTION (plucks, marimba, piano ostinato), and a near-zero transient rate now disqualifies a track instead of recommending it.
  • Two rounds of free-library beds failed on the MIX as much as the music: a flat, static bed at -31 LUFS (LUFS is how loud something FEELS over time, so -31 is very far under the -16 narrator) under wall-to-wall narration is inaudible by construction. Rule (the route that finally passed): generate the bed, give it music-only holds at act turns, and duck it against the narration instead of burying it.
  • Preview clips stopped matching the final render because amix by default quietly drops the narration about 6 dB. Rule: normalize=0 is load-bearing in the preview command.
  • A track shorter than the composition has to loop and the seam shows. Rule: prefer a track longer than the video (the v1.5 pick was 209.5s under a 203s composition), or cross-fade a loop deliberately before baking.
  • Eight of nine paid Lyria takes died inside a session scratchpad and cannot be regenerated (the model is non-deterministic). Rule: every paid take, winner or not, is copied into the durable music-library/ at generation time, and new videos check that library before paying again.
  • Adding the end card grew the total length and left the card silent because the bed was still cut to the old length. Rule: after TOTAL changes, re-bake the bed with its fade ending at the new total.
  • Verification was being done on the ingredients rather than the result. Rule: measure the finished mp4 (music-only window about -35 dB mean, speech about -16 dB); music-only above about -30 dB means re-bake, never patch the level in the composition.
  • Sources: music-beds.md, motion-rules.md, tts-voice-law.md.

Gate

GATE 3: Igor listens to three previews and picks one. He is choosing the emotional register of the whole video, not just a track. A good answer names one of the three. The precedent for rejecting all three is v1.4, where his one-line "yoga class" verdict was aimed at the register (a sustained pad) rather than at that particular track, and it rewrote the brief to quiet motion. The three preview files are sent to him directly, since the working folder is not somewhere he can reach, each labelled with its register and track name.

After he picks, that track is baked against the actual narration: low rumble filtered out below 240 Hz, the level set to about -19 LUFS, then ducked automatically so the music dips whenever the narrator speaks and rises again in the gaps, with the fades ending exactly at the video's total length. The ducking and the music-only holds are baked into the file itself, so what he previews is exactly what renders. The result is then measured on the finished video to confirm the music-only stretches sit far below the speech, and the track's name, author and licence are recorded in the project notes. The old route, a static bed at a fixed -31 LUFS with no ducking, is superseded: it is what made two rounds of candidates inaudible.

8 Companion pagevideo first, sticky while playing No Igor gate

What happens

If the video gets a web page, the page is built video first: masthead, a short lead-in, then the video, then the headline and the article body. While the video is playing it sticks to the top of the screen as you scroll, and releases when you pause or it ends. The page ships the video, a subtitle file kept separate rather than burned into the picture, a freshly captured poster image and the logo.

Tools that run

  • knowledge/page-patterns.md the layout and code patterns
  • python3 -m http.server 8765 serves the page locally, which is what actually runs the server
  • .claude/launch.json attaches the preview pane to that local server (a config with a url and no command attaches)
  • getBoundingClientRect measurements checks real positions while scrolling

What can go wrong

  • Checking the page by opening the file directly instead of through a local server, which shows a static snapshot with none of the behaviour running.
  • Leaving the video section nested inside another wrapper, which silently breaks the stick-while-playing behaviour.
  • Trusting screenshots. The preview pane can paint stale images, so positions are measured in the page instead.
Engineering rules, for the record
  • Every single "the sticky player is broken" report turned out to be the page opened as a file:// snapshot in the app browser, where no JS runs at all. Rule: serve it over localhost before believing any behaviour report.
  • The player was left inside its own <section> and un-stuck immediately, because a sticky element is constrained by its direct parent. Rule: flatten the video out of its section into the wrapper that spans the video plus the following sections.
  • A hardcoded 86px sticky offset overlapped the masthead at phone width, where the mast wraps to two or three rows. Rule: measure mast height plus 24px into a CSS variable on load and on resize.
  • Screenshots from the hidden browser pane came back stale after a resize and contradicted the real geometry. Rule: a screenshot that disagrees with a getBoundingClientRect() reading is the screenshot being wrong, re-measure rather than re-shoot.
  • With the headline above it, the player sat a third of the way down the page. Rule: kicker, then video, then headline and intro below it; and the 52px section rhythm gets cut to 16px directly under the mast because it otherwise reads as a hole.
  • A stale poster image fails silently until somebody loads the page cold. Rule: re-cut the poster frame from the final render on EVERY re-render.
  • Source: page-patterns.md.

Gate

No Igor gate, and nothing to verify here. Runs start to finish on its own.

9 Shipfingerprint both sides, then live check Gate 4

What happens

Every file is fingerprinted on both sides before it is committed, so nothing that Drive quietly altered slips through. The video and the page go out as one commit, tagged with a version number. Once the automated publish finishes, the live video is downloaded back down from the public URL and fingerprinted against the local copy, and the live page is checked for a marker string unique to this release.

Tools that run

  • md5 <scratchpad file> <repo file> fingerprints each file both sides
  • git push origin HEAD:main --tags publishes video and page together
  • gh run watch waits for the publish to finish
  • curl plus md5 on the live file proves the live copy matches

What can go wrong

  • Calling it done before the live check. Done means pushed, live fingerprint matched, and the exact link in Igor's hand.
  • Splitting the video and the page across two commits instead of one.
Engineering rules, for the record
  • Files edited inside a Google Drive-backed checkout report success and then silently revert minutes later, and node_modules inside Drive stalls the machine. Rule: build in the session scratchpad, copy into Drive one file at a time at ship time, with an md5 check on both copies.
  • "Shipped" has meant a link that was not live, or a live mp4 that differed from the render that was verified. Rule: one commit covering video and page, tags, push, watch the deploy, then curl the LIVE mp4 and match its md5 to the local render and grep the live page for a marker string, and it is not done until the exact link is in Igor's hand.
  • Source: gotchas.md.

Gate

GATE 4: shipping authority. Igor decides this once, in advance, when he approves the plan. If the plan he approved ends with the video going live, it ships without another ask; parking the push at that point just makes him do Claude's job. A good answer states plainly whether the plan ends with publication, and Claude pauses only if the release contains something outward-facing he has not already sanctioned. The consequence to be clear about: by default nobody shows him the finished video before the public sees it. Asking for a viewing at the plan stage is what changes that.

The task list for the shipping project is brought up to date: only work that is provably finished gets closed, and no new tasks are invented. The project's own notes, its readme, the memory file and a handoff document for the next session are all refreshed, and the code knowledge map is rebuilt. Anything learned the hard way this time is written back into the Video Factory knowledge files so the next video starts ahead of where this one did.

Cheat sheet

How to steer

Four moments, four answers. Each gate closes fastest when the answer contains the thing the gate is actually asking for, so these are phrases that can be said as they stand.

Gate 1, Stage 1, Script

The exact words, permanently

  • "Yes, those are the words, generate the take."
  • "Scene 4 line 2 should say this instead: ..."
  • "Change nothing else, only that line, then go."

Gate 2, Stage 3, Cut and pace

Pace against length

  • "Natural pace at 3m15, ship that one."
  • "Take the 10 percent faster cut at 3m00."
  • "Give me both real durations before I pick."

Gate 3, Stage 7, Music

The register, not just a track

  • "Preview two, the cinematic one."
  • "None of the three, the register is wrong, go quiet motion."
  • "Send the previews with a music only hold in the slice."

Gate 4, Stage 9, Ship

Shipping authority, decided in advance

  • "The plan ends live, ship it without asking again."
  • "Build it and stop before the push, I want to see it first."
  • "Anything outward facing I have not sanctioned, ask me."

Non negotiable

Hard rules in plain words

The nine rules the pipeline will not break, each earned by something going wrong before.

  1. One voice recording, made in one go, measured before it is used. Never recorded line by line, never stretched line by line, never shipped unmeasured. Slowing individual scenes cut out of that one recording is allowed and capped, because the voice cannot drift inside a single performance.
  2. The video is built from the real product's own stylesheet, so what you see is the product, not a drawing of it.
  3. Every number on screen is calculated by the product's own code. Anything that cannot be is labelled proposed or illustrative on screen.
  4. Never edit the generated file. Edit the template and rebuild.
  5. Build in a temporary working folder and copy into Google Drive with a fingerprint check, because Drive reverts edits made in place.
  6. Music ships already mixed, ducked under the voice, with its fades baked into the file, and Igor picks the track from measured previews, so what he previews is what renders.
  7. Four things need Igor's sign-off: script wording, the pace against length trade-off, the music pick, and anything outward-facing. Shipped means pushed, live-verified, and the exact link in his hand.
  8. No school logos or school names on screen in outward-facing videos, even where the product itself shows them. Neutral product styling stays.
  9. Sweep the script for words that change meaning with stress before recording (present, live, read, record, project), because one wrong reading costs the entire recording. The final spoken phrase also gets its visual inside the closing scene, not only on the end card.

The words used here

Glossary

Everything the pipeline says out loud, in plain terms.

acrossfade (ffmpeg's name for a crossfade) = joining a short track to a copy of itself with a smooth overlap so a looped bed has no audible seam.

adj_mean / adj_max = two numbers from the take score. adj_mean is how different neighbouring scenes sound on average; adj_max is the worst single pair. A worst pair two or three times the average means one scene jumps, so the recording is thrown away.

amix / normalize=0 = the command that mixes two audio tracks together; without normalize=0 it quietly drops the narration about 6 dB and the preview stops matching the final render.

at() / atf() = the two ways to time a cue: at is a fixed number of seconds into a line, atf is a fraction of the line, so it still lands correctly if the line gets shorter.

atempo = the ffmpeg speed knob for audio; it MULTIPLIES speed, so slowing down needs a number below 1 (192 wpm to 167 wpm is 0.87).

bpm = beats per minute, the tempo of a music track.

clip_media_fit = the harmless warning that an audio clip is shorter than the slot it sits in, normal here because of the lead, tail and silent end card.

companion page = the web page that carries the video plus the same argument in readable form for people who never press play.

cue = the instruction that fires an on-screen animation at a specific moment of the narration.

dB (decibel) = the unit for "how much louder or quieter"; small numbers matter here, 1.6 dB is how much the same voice differs from itself, while 3.62 dB between neighbouring scenes was audible enough to throw a take away.

deterministic render = the same input always producing the identical output frames, which is why random number generators are banned in the visuals.

drift = the voice slowly changing character across the video, or jumping between scenes, because the model re-invents the speaker each time it is asked.

drift EQ (deflutter) = a gentle tone correction that flattens that slow change; it alters colour only, never timing, and must be run exactly once.

duck-mix = automatically dipping the music whenever the narrator speaks and letting it rise again in the gaps, instead of leaving the music permanently quiet.

end card = the silent held card after the last spoken word that points viewers at the companion page; adding it lengthens the video and forces a music re-bake.

ffmpeg / ffprobe = the command-line tools that cut, mix, measure and convert audio and video; ffprobe reads properties, ffmpeg does the work.

file:// = opening a page as a local file rather than through a server; scripts do not run that way, which is why "broken" reports were false alarms.

formants = the resonances that give a voice its identity; stretching by different amounts smears them, so the voice stops sounding like one person.

freeze-safe = the rule that if animation never runs, the frozen frame still looks finished, because the resting state IS the final state.

Gemini / Charon = the text-to-speech model in use and the specific narrator voice chosen from it.

getBoundingClientRect = the browser measurement of exactly where an element sits on screen, trusted over screenshots.

GSAP = the animation library driving the on-screen motion timeline.

headless Chrome = a browser running invisibly for automated screenshots and rendering; it refuses to lay out narrower than 500px.

heteronym = a word whose meaning changes with where the stress falls (PRESent versus preSENT, live, read, record, project); the model can pick the wrong reading and force a full retake, so these get reworded first.

highpass = a filter that removes the low rumble below a set frequency (240 Hz for the bed), so the music stops crowding the voice.

holds = deliberate silent gaps in the narration (cold open, act turns, end card) that exist so the music can be heard on its own for a couple of seconds.

immediateRender:false = the flag that stops the animation library from applying a "start" state at time zero and leaving the element looking wrong until its cue.

LEAD / TAIL = the silence deliberately left at the start and end of the narration track.

ledger / provenance = the written record of where an asset came from (track name, author, id, licence, what was done to it), kept in the shipping README.

lint / check = the automatic pre-render inspection for overlapping clips, layout problems and contrast issues.

loudnorm = the ffmpeg filter that normalises a file to a target loudness (LUFS), used to set the narrator level and the bed level.

LUFS = how loud a track FEELS to a human ear over time, not its peak level. The narrator sits at -16 LUFS. The bed is now baked at about -19 LUFS and ducked under the voice; the old fixed -31 LUFS bed, about 15 dB underneath and never ducked, is superseded because it could not be heard.

Lyria = the paid Google model used to generate custom music beds; its output is non-deterministic, so every take must be saved.

md5 = a short fingerprint of a file's contents; two files with the same md5 are identical, which is how a live upload is proved to match the local render.

pace cap = the safety net at stage 3: any scene reading faster than 192 words a minute is slowed back to that ceiling, never below 0.84 of its speed, and only ever downward. It exists because the voice engine accelerates through a long recording.

phonetic floor = how much the same voice differs from itself just because the words are different (about 1.6 dB on this script). Neighbouring scenes have to sit below it to count as the same person.

poster frame = the still image shown before the video starts, cut from the final render so the still and the first frame match.

register (music) = the overall character or mood family of a track (upbeat corporate, minimal tech pulse, cinematic documentary), each with its own tempo and instruments.

register (voice) = the pitch and tone level a narrator settles into; a "register shift" is the audible jump that got two versions rejected.

score_take.py = the script that scores a recording and prints the accept-or-regenerate numbers; running it is not optional.

scratchpad = the temporary working folder outside Google Drive where all building happens, because Drive silently reverts edits made in place.

split.mjs = the script that cuts the one long recording into scenes, sets the speed, applies the pace cap and writes the timings.

seek = jumping the video to an arbitrary moment; everything must draw correctly from a cold jump, which is why nothing may depend on animation having already played.

sidechain (sidechaincompress) = the tool that does the ducking: it listens to the narration track and turns the music down in response to it.

spectral / timbre = the "colour" of a sound, meaning which frequencies it is made of, measured here to prove two scenes sound like the same person.

spectral centroid = the brightness measure of a sound, the average frequency where its energy sits; a big spread between scenes means the voice wandered.

srt = a plain-text subtitle file with timings, shipped alongside the video rather than burned into the picture.

stagger = starting several similar animations one after another with a fixed delay, so items arrive in sequence rather than all at once.

sticky = a CSS behaviour that pins an element in place while the page scrolls; here the player pins under the masthead only while it is playing.

take = one complete generated recording of the whole script, produced by a single request; the unit that gets accepted or thrown away.

time-stretch = making audio longer or shorter without changing its pitch; doing it to lines that were recorded separately is what made the narrator sound like two people. Slowing scenes cut out of ONE recording does not carry that risk.

timings.json = the generated file that records where every scene starts and how long it lasts; the animation cues read from it, and it is never hand-edited.

TOTAL = the full length of the finished composition in seconds; when it changes, the music bed and its fade have to be rebuilt to match.

transient rate (onsets per second) = how often new notes start; a sustained pad scores near zero, which now disqualifies it as "sleepy".

TTS = text to speech, the model that turns the written script into the narrator's voice.

volumedetect = the quick ffmpeg meter used to check whether a region of the finished video is at the expected level.

worktree = a second working copy of the same repository, used so ship-time work is isolated and pinned to the current main branch.

wpm = words per minute, the reading pace of the narration; the target is 165 with an accept band of 152 to 178.
